Taiwan · Nanjing · Yunnan

Pack for three climates

September is still summer in Taiwan, transitional and muggy in Nanjing, and wet with sharp elevation changes in Yunnan.

01

One week

Taiwan

Hot · humid · storm-aware

What September feels like

Expect summer clothing, strong sun, humidity, sudden downpours, and air-conditioning. Taiwan’s typhoon season generally runs through September, so every outdoor day needs an indoor fallback.

Breathable layersRain coverSun hatMosquito protection

With Zuzu

  • Use metro and rail as the default; save car-seat installation for planned transfers.
  • Carry the Aer+ onto the plane only if the operating carrier confirms it.
  • Keep one dry outfit and rain cover accessible—not buried in checked luggage.
  • Build heat breaks into 11 a.m.–3 p.m.; convenience stores make easy cooling and diaper stops.
Car-seat rule: Taiwan requires a rear-facing restraint for children under two in private and rental cars. Commercial passenger cars are excluded from that rule’s definition, but taxi exemption is not a safety substitute.

Nanjing

What September feels like

Early September can still feel like summer; typical daytime temperatures ease from about 30°C to 27°C across the month, with warm nights. Humidity and rain remain plausible, so quick-dry fabrics beat heavy cotton.

Quick-dry topsSandalsLight rain shellIndoor layer

With Zuzu

  • Start parks, walls, and outdoor sights early; reserve museums and malls for the hottest hours.
  • Carry more water than the temperature alone suggests.
  • Use the stroller canopy for shade, but check airflow and her temperature frequently.
  • Choose lodging near metro access and a convenience store; short returns protect naps.

The layer system

Pack one breathable base, one fleece, and one rain shell per person. Add a light puffer for Zuzu if Shangri-La or other high areas are included. Keep socks and a dry layer in the day bag.

FleeceRain shellWarm sleep sackSun protection

Altitude with a 16-month-old

  • Stage the ascent—Kunming, then Dali/Lijiang, then higher areas.
  • Make the first 48 hours at a new high elevation gentle.
  • Watch for unexplained fussiness, poor appetite, pale appearance, sleep change, or reduced activity.
  • Do not ascend higher with symptoms; worsening symptoms require descent and medical help.
Important: Young children are as susceptible to altitude illness as adults but cannot describe headache or nausea. Discuss any Shangri-La/high-altitude plan with Zuzu’s pediatrician.

Shopping assessment · updated August 19, 2026

Comfort first. Then carry burden. Then installation.

Zuzu should still ride rear-facing. Your priorities rule out a simple “pick the lightest” answer: the best travel seat is one she tolerates, both parents can carry, and both parents can install correctly in an unfamiliar vehicle.

01 · highest weight

Child comfort

A calmer child matters on long drives and flight days. Padding, usable recline, harness fit, and Zuzu’s own reaction outrank marketing copy.

02

Pack + carry

Weight matters every transfer. “Fits in luggage” is a physical test with your exact suitcase—not a claim any shortlisted convertible seat can promise.

03

Install well

No-rethread adjustment, accessible belt paths, and a readable level reduce friction. A practiced basic seat still beats an unfamiliar premium one.

The two-seat audition

  1. Measure Zuzu’s standing height, seated shoulder height, and weight.
  2. Find display models or sellers whose written return policy allows a careful fit trial.
  3. Give each a calm 30-minute sit; take only a purchased keeper on a real nap-length drive. Never add unapproved padding.
  4. Install each rear-facing with a seat belt in two cars; both David and Luna must get less than one inch of movement at the belt path.
  5. Place each in your actual suitcase or carry rig, then walk it around the block.
  6. Choose the better whole-system fit and book a CPST check with the winner.

The packing truth

No magic folding rear-facing seat—yet.

The genuinely folding option in this market, the WAYB Pico, is forward-facing only and specifies age 2+. That makes it inappropriate for Zuzu now. Shell shape matters as much as published dimensions, so only an at-home suitcase test can answer the luggage question.

If the Contender is too cumbersome, the 8 lb Scenera Extend becomes the rational fallback—but its basic padding and more finicky rear seat-belt installation directly trade against your first and third priorities.

“Comfort signal” is a feature-based research judgment, not a lab measurement. Zuzu’s own fit test decides it. All U.S.-sold seats must meet federal standards; NHTSA ease-of-use ratings describe usability, not comparative safety.

01

Why not rent one there?

You may not know its crash history, manual, cleanliness, fit, or installation. A known seat is valuable when every vehicle is unfamiliar.

02

Why rear-facing?

AAP guidance is to remain rear-facing until the seat’s height or weight limit. Zuzu’s age alone is not a reason to turn her.

03

What about the airplane?

If Zuzu has her own seat, use the approved restraint onboard. Confirm foreign-airline policy, seat width, allowed location, and orientation in writing.

04

What about taxis?

Prebook when possible, request an accessible rear belt, allow installation time, and decline vehicles that cannot take the seat securely.

The home test

Do this twice before the airport.

  1. Read the entire manual and locate the aircraft-certification label.
  2. Fit the harness to Zuzu while everyone is calm.
  3. Install rear-facing with a lap-and-shoulder belt, without LATCH.
  4. Practice with the vehicle belt’s locking method; use a locking clip only when the manual permits and calls for it.
  5. Verify less than one inch of movement at the belt path.
  6. Time a clean removal and reinstall. Aim for confidence, not speed.
  7. Book a CPST check with the actual travel seat before departure.
